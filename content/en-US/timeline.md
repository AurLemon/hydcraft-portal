How did HydCraft end up where it is today? If I really had to get into it, I still could not give you some grand narrative. The server never had a blueprint. It just muddled its way here through one round after another of positive and negative feedback. Since 2018, the server has made it through seven seasons, and Season 8 is almost here.

It started as a few friends playing together, but that manic phase where people stayed up every night building giant houses and claiming land for huge projects is long gone. Season 7 has been running since August 2023, and the pace has visibly slowed. People have school, jobs, and, frankly, I as the owner am not really capable of leading big builds anymore. Instead of logging in and AFKing on the server, people now spend more time chatting in the group or scrolling through old screenshots and reminiscing. Still, the server has not completely stalled. It is still nice to tinker with things once in a while, and Season 7 has produced quite a few results too. You can see some of them on the intro page.

Oh, right, I should introduce myself first. I am the server owner, Aurora_Lemon. People usually call me Lemon. From here on, I will record what this server has actually gone through over the years from a first-person point of view.

## The Beginning

Compared with what it is now, the earliest version of the server was almost a completely different thing. At the end of August 2018, a friend of mine pulled a few people, including me, into an RTM multiplayer session through an RTM group chat. I knew a bit of tech at the time, so I casually tweaked the server and got it running.

On September 1, 2018, that multiplayer server officially went live, and other people could finally join. Season 1 was on version 1.7.10, with RTM plus RC. Most of the earliest players came from RTM Baidu Tieba group chats. Around the spawn point, players gradually built all kinds of structures, roads, stations, and early urban rail transit, in an area that was later named Binhai City. At the very beginning, there was no complete system and no clear goal. It was simply a few people starting to play together, and the server's name and website both came later, bit by bit.

In early September, people saw that I knew how to handle server-side and technical stuff, so the person who had started the multiplayer session handed me the title of owner. I scraped together some money, bought a panel host on Taobao, and put the server on it for everyone to play. I was not even the group owner yet at that point. That was transferred to me a month or two later.

The server was originally called the Binhai City Server. In November 2018, someone joked in a group chat, "OnePlus has a phone OS called Hydrogen OS. Why not call it that?" I later adopted that offhand joke as the server's name, first as Hydrogen Server and then, a few days later, as HydCraft. The Binhai City Server was officially renamed the HydCraft Server, and that name has stayed ever since.

The server logo was also drawn in November 2018. A manager from an MC studio I was in at the time drew it for me, what is now the O2 team. The current logo is just a variation of that early one with a different color scheme. In any case, our server has absolutely nothing to do with chemistry.

That is enough background. Back to the server not long after September 1.

By the end of September, everyone felt we had already played that save into the ground, so we wanted to wipe it and start over. That restart was also when we formally started using the word "season." I am not even completely sure what the word technically means. I think it comes from Japanese. Either way, it is basically similar to a season in the sense of "which numbered save file" in MC server circles.

Season 1 lasted only about a month. The server first ran on a home machine, and both the network and the performance were unstable. The experience only improved a little after I moved it to the panel host. That phase was still mostly just playing, casually playing. The principle of doing whatever we felt like doing was already set back then.

When Season 2 began in October, the player count slowly grew. Most newcomers came either from friends inviting friends or from us shamelessly sneaking promotion into other server groups. At that point, the group already had roughly 30 to 40 people. More names also started appearing on the map. Binhai City, Kunlun City, Xijing City, Fuyuki City, Dongfeng City, Hokkai City, Pingguo City. Those places all emerged one after another during that period.

Even so, calling them cities is only half true. A lot of them were really just pieces of land one or two people had kept up for a long time. They had their own routes, architectural styles, and networks of acquaintances. People started claiming land, connecting roads, and arguing about whose plan made more sense. The map was still the same map, but things were no longer just "let's open a server and play together." Once there are more people, those kinds of problems show up.

Many conflicts looked like construction issues on the surface, but underneath they were still people issues. Who was willing to build roads together, who did not want rail lines crossing their own territory, who disliked someone else's building style, who said a few emotionally charged things in the group chat. In the end, all of that could directly affect what happened in the game. Looking back now, you can already see the shadow of many later problems in Season 2. That is simply what comes with choosing a community path.

Season 2 actually lasted quite a while. I remember Lunar New Year in 2019 as the liveliest Lunar New Year I personally experienced on the server. There were not that many people, but it was fun. By the summer of 2019, we were already close to 50 people. A lot also happened during Season 2. We had more contact with other servers, and plenty of disputes surfaced. What do you do when disputes cannot be resolved? I did not know either. People left group chats, fought, dragged arguments into other servers. It was all these trivial, messy little things.

Still, those were not the real reason Season 2 stopped. It stopped because of something much more absurd. It was not a graceful wrap-up. The free hosting provider simply ran off. The server cut out just like that, and time froze on May 7, 2019. Why? In February 2019, several other managers, trying to save money, had moved the server through some channel onto a foreign guy's free machine host. But the most expensive thing is always the free thing. On May 7, the guy said he had developed a serious illness, needed multiple organ transplants, mentally broke down, and took back all of the machines.

::image-grid{class="grid-cols-1" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_3/lighting_1.webp","caption":"Aerial view of HydCore Factory 401 in Season 3","height":"18rem"}]'}
::

Then came the story of Season 3. At that point everyone thought this would not do, so after the free provider disappeared, one player proposed that we pool money and buy a self-managed machine. We really did scrape together a machine worth only a few hundred yuan, and Season 3 started up again in the middle of May. Season 3 moved from 1.7.10 to 1.12.2 and used a more diverse set of mods.

Then something even more absurd happened. Two months later, on July 23, the player responsible for the host could no longer maintain it for personal reasons, so the machine had to be sold and the money returned. Later, during a system reinstall, the player handling operations at the time lacked migration and backup experience, so the drive was formatted directly and the Season 3 save vanished with it.

Season 3 ended just as muddled as that. If there were an IF route where everyone had decided to stop playing then, perhaps there would have been no later HydCraft story at all. Fortunately, everyone decided to keep going. Season 3 did not leave much behind, but some lessons started sticking from then on. That was when I realized that versioning, hosting, backups, and system environments are the kinds of things you cannot dodge when something really goes wrong.

From Season 1 to Season 3, it was basically one long process of trial and error. How do you open a server, how do you migrate it, how do you handle mod problems, what do you preserve first when something breaks. We did not figure those things out beforehand. We learned them slowly after falling into pits. On top of that, I had to learn how to choose people, judge people, and place people. I could not keep fantasizing about being a hands-off owner. I needed to know every link in the chain myself instead of tossing everything to someone else. People argued after the server broke too, but dwelling on whose fault it was had no meaning and no use. First get the server back up, then talk about responsibility.

## Taking Shape

::image-grid{class="grid-cols-1 sm:grid-cols-2" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_4/lighting_1.webp","caption":"Aerial view of Beihu City, Qingcheng Metropolis in Season 4"},{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_4/lighting_2.webp","caption":"Aerial view of Old Nanchuan in Season 4"},{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_4/lighting_3.webp","caption":"Aerial view of New Nanchuan in Season 4"},{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_4/lighting_4.webp","caption":"Aerial view of Kiyosumi City in Season 4"}]'}

On July 30, 2019, Season 4 began. By then the server was finally a bit more stable than before. Resources were less tight, there were more people, and the group had already reached 60 plus. Railways, cities, and public works started to feel more divided by teams and by individual builders. The tendency to cluster into groups was still very strong in this phase. Many cities and regions were not really governed by institutions. They were governed by whoever kept maintaining them and whose words carried more weight. Behind a place name there were usually the taste, time, and temperament of a few specific people. On the surface it looked like administrative geography. In reality it was more like a landscape of little warlords.

After online classes began during the pandemic in early 2020, that period became the craziest one yet. Nearby servers and the whole rail-transit circle were expanding like mad, and all kinds of unbelievable things happened. You can treat it as kids playing house and getting into group fights. We were affected by it too, and at one point there was even a reorganization of the administrative committee.

There was even a manager who revived a branch server from the Season 2 save, what people later called the "Daoming faction," even though the administrative committee knew nothing about that branch server at the time. After a while, that branch server became independent outright. Why revive an old save at all? Because that manager's computer specs were too weak for him to get into Seasons 3 and 4 at all.

Still, everyone had a lot more time to stay online, and the server obviously heated up. It was not only HydCraft. Many servers were like that at the time. People were stuck in front of screens, and it became easier to pour time into the game. During that period, highways, public works, city construction, administrative boundary adjustments, and management structure changes all moved forward a lot. We also had much more contact with neighboring servers.

What I remember most vividly about Season 4 all happened during the 2020 online-class period. The only pre-pandemic event that really left a mark on me was a core manager leaving in September 2019. That incident hit me hard. I still do not know why I found it so difficult to accept someone leaving. I tried to reason with them in my own way, but it did not work. That was when I learned that some outcomes simply cannot be changed by me. Maybe my yandere streak was already showing back then.

Season 4 also produced one especially symbolic result: the server started to feel like a small community. Not one small group, but a cluster of small groups. Conflicts and personnel changes never went away, and in this phase they were actually more intense, just not as openly violent as in Seasons 1 and 2. In terms of construction, players no longer logged in just to build casually for a bit. They started building supporting infrastructure around cities, roads, and railways. There was even subcontracting and outsourcing.

Many players on the server were growing up along with the server itself. Back then most of the server was made up of middle school and high school students. After Seasons 1, 2, and 3, we had finally reached a stage where we could really play and really build properly. Everyone could finally breathe a little.

## A Different Summer

::image-grid{class="grid-cols-1 sm:grid-cols-2" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_5/lighting_1.webp","caption":"Aerial view of Guangyang District, Yuanjiang Metropolis in Season 5"},{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_5/lighting_2.webp","caption":"Aerial view of Old Xihai in Season 5"},{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_5/lighting_3.webp","caption":"Aerial view of New Xihai in Season 5"},{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_5/lighting_4.webp","caption":"A train crossing a bridge near a station in Hokkai in Season 5"}]'}
::

In May 2020, after everyone had built like maniacs during the online-class period, we realized the problems were still too big, so we agreed to wind down Season 4 smoothly and open a new season. Season 5 officially began on June 7. The idea behind opening it was simple. The previous season had become a bit messy toward the end, so everyone wanted a new map, a new pace, and a reshuffle.

Season 5 was still a very typical acquaintance community, and no one was trying to turn it into some highly institutionalized giant server. What people cared about was very simple: whether we could have fun together and whether we could leave our own mark on the map.

::image-grid{defaultHeight="20rem" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_5/misc_division.webp","caption":"Season 5 administrative divisions","width":"18rem"},{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_5/misc_sports_game.webp","caption":"The first HydCraft Games","width":"14rem"}]'}
::

What Season 5 left for many people was mainly liveliness. It was the first time it had been that lively.

The summer of 2020 was the first peak. Tokiwa, Yuanjiang, and Kiyosumi were all seeing major construction. A whole bunch of TV stations popped up on the server, and many people who liked broadcast-style play came in. The three current managers all took part in that wild HydCraft broadcasting craze at the time. The founding ceremony of the Argon Autonomous Region, which was dedicated to survival play, pushed that summer to its peak. At one point there were more than 15 people online at the same time.

The summer of 2021 heated up again. Influenced by the atmosphere of the Tokyo Olympics, we even held a sports meet on the server that summer. The sports meet itself was chaotic and the rules were far from rigorous, but when people later brought it up, they always remembered the concrete little moments first. That was arguably HydCraft's first shared memory as a community.

Another incident was when the server was temporarily down for a week because a provider ran off. At the time I pessimistically wondered whether we were about to be forced into Season 6. I stared at a Remote Desktop connection timing out and fell into thought. Then the story took a dramatic turn. The new provider found us through the website listed in the server announcement. He told us that our original provider owed him money, so he had no choice but to pull the network cable. In the end, he promised we could migrate straight onto his machines, and as for the debt, he would sue on his own.

By the way, HydCraft Wiki also started being maintained during Season 5. And the buildings? Honestly, there still was not some especially dazzling breakthrough there. Back then, what people in the circle thought of when HydCraft came up was a good atmosphere and maybe a few too many arguments. If I have to say what Season 5 left behind, I still would not call it "scale." I would call it the clearly multiplied shared memories from that period. The lively summers, the sports meet, the jokes in the group, and the increasingly smooth tacit understanding between people. When many veteran players think of HydCraft, that period is what jumps out first.

After September 2021, Season 5 slowly cooled down again. Player enthusiasm was changing, the RTM version ecosystem had stalled, and people kept calling for a new season, so the old model's fatigue became more and more obvious. The new server later opened under the name "Helium branch server" was in practice the predecessor of Season 6. Looking back now, Season 5 feels like the last truly lively major phase of the RTM era. It may not have been the most mature one, but it is certainly one of the easiest to remember.

## Wind on the Island

In January 2022, a player in the group began recommending a more modern railway mod, the one now widely known as MTR. After a week or two of arguing, I accepted it. A former manager who had once opened a branch server with the Season 2 save and later gone independent even suggested that I upgrade and said he could merge his server back with ours, though I do not think that would have meant much. Season 6, or rather this new ecosystem carrying everyone's new hopes, came into being like that.

To avoid disrupting Season 5, I still played it cautiously and launched it under the name "Helium branch server" on February 17, 2022, running it in parallel with Season 5 for a while. That parallel period did not last long. A month later, Season 5 was shut down because the maintenance cost had become too high, and the only thing that really survived was this branch server, which later officially counted as Season 6.

Season 6 moved to 1.16.5 and used new mods such as MTR and Create. Compared with the old RTM setup, the railway experience became much smoother, and everyone suddenly had a lot more ideas about cities and routes. Season 6 used realistic terrain generated by mods, and everyone was concentrated on one giant island, which made construction even more concentrated.

Season 6 is the part I remember most vividly. It was also the busiest, most crowded, and most eventful phase. The island map pulled people closer together. Places like Nanyu, Haikang, Hengdan, and Jiuxiang became lively very quickly. Add videos, publicity, summer activities, and invitations through acquaintances, and there were always people on the server during that period.

Its underlying tone still felt like a "large friends-only server." It was not held together by commercialization or heavy traffic acquisition, but by shared interests, social ties, and friends bringing friends. Many people came here not only because they wanted to build railways or cities, but because they wanted to enter a place that was lively, full of inside jokes, and easy to talk in.

Once the player count went up, though, problems piled up very quickly too. By then, the group was already close to 180 plus people. The island map concentrated construction, so it also concentrated spatial conflict. Routes sprawling in every direction, regions gradually filling up, people refusing to let railways pass through their territory. All of that happened in Season 6. On the surface people were arguing about where railways should go, whether a building should be approved, or whether terrain could be changed. In reality the arguments were about boundaries, trust, and who got to decide.

Many regions were administrative districts in name, but in practice they were more like territory long managed by different players or small circles. Everyone had their own aesthetics, routes, and social networks, and once those collided, approvals, public space, and route conflicts stopped being ordinary "construction details."

To keep the chaos down, we later tried project approvals, a community platform, specialized review bodies, and more detailed building rules. I cannot say the ideas were entirely wrong, but once they were put into practice, the problems became obvious too. Once the hierarchy gets deeper and the process gets rigid, what was originally just a construction disagreement in a small community can very easily turn into personal resentment.

What Season 6 left behind was not just liveliness, but also some very concrete lessons. Small communities are not necessarily suited to complex institutions, and approvals are not some universal cure. In the end, what really works is usually a small number of people willing to take responsibility. What is operating is people, not some system or institution.

Leaving the sad parts aside for a moment, Season 6 also left a lot of memes behind. The compatibility issue between MTR and OptiFine later came to be known as CCL. Players would walk into certain areas, suddenly get rendering errors, and watch chunks stop loading until they quit and rejoined the game. Later on, some people even deliberately imitated the error format just to scare others.

The collective spamming of "1" after a WorldEdit command froze the server's main thread also became part of how people made bitter fun of the time. It was not just heckling. When the main thread froze, player movement, block updates, and most in-game actions all stopped, but chat could sometimes still go through. So everyone kept sending "1" to confirm whether the server was still alive. If chat still came through, that at least meant the server had not completely died yet. If even chat was gone, that usually meant the save might already be too late to preserve, and people would start worrying about rollbacks next.

Back then we also held "discussion meetings." In reality they were just Tencent Meetings where we chatted and, truthfully, did not reach many results. At the time I really believed that if we just held enough meetings and made the systems detailed enough, many old problems could be solved all at once. Looking back now, that was too simple a way of thinking. It is not that those discussions were entirely useless. It is just that they could not solve as much as we thought they could. In an acquaintance community, manpower, execution cost, and relationship complexity are always there. In the end, many meetings were really just a bunch of people seriously imagining what the server might become in the future.

For me, Season 6 holds both good memories and bad ones. Old friends left. Managers left because of factional disputes. People formed cliques, excluded others, and attacked people recklessly. All of that happened in 2023. For me, 2023 is not a good number. I never expected such a small online community to end up carrying all of my emotions. I lacked a sense of security too badly. I wanted to keep everyone, and in the end I pleased neither side and kept no one. I will not write those stories into the server history here, though. I will write them on my personal website instead, maybe as the [owner's postscript](https://aurlemon.top/en-US/journey).

In the summer of 2023, construction started to saturate, small-group conflicts and questions about direction all exploded at once, and Season 6 basically entered its late stage then. Season 7 was opened after that pile of problems, so of course it was a new map and new technology, but it also carried a response to the late-Season-6 problems with it. Later on, Season 6 would occasionally reopen. It was not the most stable period, but if I had to pick the phase that was the most crowded, the most vivid, and the clearest example of community complexity, I would still probably pick Season 6. It was only near the end of Season 6 that I truly realized we had to explicitly forbid clique-building and excluding other people.

## Still Glowing

On August 7, 2023, Season 7 officially began. The aftereffect of Season 6 had not fully faded, so the new season naturally carried a bit of a "let's move somewhere else and start over" feeling. The map was drawn by players themselves, and the person who led that map work later became the current vice owner. The version moved up to 1.18.2, and the mod ecosystem shifted toward a newer stack as well. On December 31, 2025, Season 7 was upgraded again to 1.20.1 while preserving the save.

The early preparation for Season 7 relied heavily on players pushing it forward themselves. The map, the test server, and the client pack integration were not things someone assigned from the top and got done. A few core players just pushed them through on their own. I only realized another new season was really happening after I received the server folder later on.

During the preparation for Season 7, something else rather interesting happened.

At the time, we were drawing the terrain for the new Season 7 save when a player from within the server came to us and wanted to discuss a collaboration. His idea was to stitch the save of his already closed server together with the new terrain we were preparing for Season 7 and use them in a joint form. The arrangement was not meant as an annexation. It was supposed to be a relatively equal rebuilding of the management team under a new name.

I refused that proposal, of course. The demands were too strict, and we had no need to merge. He wanted to revive his old server, and he had his own shared memories there, but we were not dead yet. We had our own memories too. Still, as the owner, I could understand how he felt. Once a server closes, the buildings inside it, the relationships between its players, and the effort once poured into it do not lose their meaning immediately. I heard he later achieved something similar through another joint project, so I should congratulate him here as well.

Once Season 7 actually got going, though, the overall pace clearly slowed down. Many veteran players started school or work, the time they could spend on MC kept shrinking, and although the map got bigger and the version got newer, construction also became more scattered. There were not enough people who could keep leading things forward over the long term, and the rate at which new players came in did not keep up either. Even so, Season 7 has produced quite a few excellent builds too, such as Ouwen, Tongyun Railway, and Guangyang.

On the eve of the Season 7 upgrade to 1.20.1, I once heard a line: "As far as I know, HydCraft's management has very serious problems." That line may have passed from one departing player to another before it reached me. My first reaction was surprise and the anger of feeling denied, but more than that, I felt helplessness and understanding.

So compared with a few years ago, HydCraft really is much quieter now. It has not ended. It has just found a different way to live. In the past, we proved we were still hot by constantly opening new projects. Now, it is more that people still remember it and still maintain it. More things are discussed inside the Elders group, and we live with one another in relative peace.

From 2023 up to now, Season 7 has not had many events that absolutely have to be written into a grand chronicle. More often, someone comes back to take a look, someone updates something in passing, or someone mentions the current state of the server in the group. It has not been as lively as Seasons 5 and 6, but it feels more like the kind of later phase a community would normally have.

If I had to find a metaphor, I would still call it a lamp. It is not as bright as it used to be, but it has never gone out. In June 2026, the Elders group suggested taking the Season 7 save straight to 1.21.1 and opening Season 8 as well. Season 8 is expected to start in mid to late July 2026.

## Acquaintance Community

::image-grid{class="grid-cols-1" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_6/player_group_20220730_202311.webp","height":"18rem"}]'}
::

The story of the seasons is over. Now it is my turn to review and sum things up. In telling the story of the seasons, I actually left out plenty of things I did not want to say and did not need to say. There were arguments and departures. Maybe they were my fault, maybe the other side's, or maybe no one was really at fault at all.

Our server has always resembled an acquaintance society, that is, a community made up of multiple small circles, and it has always been that way. A lot of MC servers that last for many years are probably like that too. At least that is how we made it through. At first we gathered through friendship and shared interests. In the middle we expanded through building, events, summer breaks, and promotion. Later on, it was more and more sustained by the long-term ties between a small group of core players.

That structure also creates warmth in a very direct way, because people here are not just pure strangers to one another. Many place names, routes, buildings, and memes all point back to specific people. A railway, a station, a memorial hall, or a map on the server is often not merely a functional facility. It is also something left behind by relationships and time.

That is also why "cities" in HydCraft have never been just administrative names on a map. Many places are really things that one player or a few players kept maintaining over a long period. They are cities, but also territories. They are administrative districts, but also circles of acquaintances. Behind a place name there might be a route plan, an architectural taste, several friendships, or an argument from long ago.

We really did have some server culture of our own as well. Early RTM and RC fixed the railway line of development in place. Season 5's sports meet became one of the first shared memories that people kept bringing up again and again. Season 6's CCL bug, WorldEdit freezes, summer building boom, player-made assets, and community platform kept stacking more layers onto those internal jokes. Many of those things were not huge in themselves, but only people who really stayed for a long time would understand them.

Of course, acquaintance society has a cost too. When relationships are close, emotions affect one another more easily. When the player base grows, problems around small groups, boundaries, and public resources become more likely. Too little structure creates chaos, but too much structure can become rigid. We tried adjusting rules, approvals, organization, and management methods at different stages, but not every attempt succeeded. Especially during the wild-growth period of Season 6, some decisions and some ways of handling small-group issues were not always satisfying.

Many people who entered HydCraft did not come through formal recruitment at all. Some were brought in by classmates. Some were pulled in by real-life friends. Some met people in other groups and were casually invited over. Some just saw screenshots, videos, or acquaintances chatting, thought it seemed interesting, and came in to take a look. Later some stayed, some played only for a short while, some went from players to group friends, and some went from group friends to names that now live only in memory.

Still, people do not stay in one place forever. In practice, someone may slowly leave on any given day. Someone stops talking and only gets noticed a week later. Someone no longer touches MC but still stays in the group chat. Someone else cannot fit into the community or cannot find their place, gradually grows quiet, and then fades out of everyone's view.

In the past, I found it very hard to accept this kind of leaving. For a while, I took every group exit and every silence very seriously. I would even keep asking people why they had left, why they had stopped talking, why they did not keep staying. Looking back now, that kind of questioning was not especially necessary, and it was not always fair either. In a community, when some people come closer, some people will inevitably leave. Not every departure needs an explanation, and not every silence means failure.

Looking back now, the state that suits HydCraft best may not be a complex institutional structure, but relationships built on trust and a sense of boundaries. A small number of people who are truly willing to take responsibility maintain the basic order. Players build and chat freely within a controllable range, and when problems appear, they are handled as calmly as possible instead of grinding every relationship through institutions again and again. Systems and rules are only the last fallback. By the time you get to that stage, things have usually already torn open.

That is also why the small group of people who are still active now feels especially important. At the moment, there are still more than forty members who stay active in the Elders group chat. For a large community, forty people might be a tiny number. But for a small community like HydCraft, these people are exactly why the server still has a voice, still has conversations, and still gets remembered.

People have said that HydCraft has a good atmosphere. I think that is probably not only because the rules are written clearly, the website looks good, or the server setup is stable enough, but because there is still a group of people willing to remain here in a gentler, lower-noise way. People may not log in every day, and there may not always be fresh building results, but as long as there are still people talking in the group chat, the save files are still preserved, the Wiki is still maintained, and old stories are still brought up from time to time, HydCraft has not truly disappeared.

At least for me, that also proves that I was never entertaining myself alone. I poured a lot of time and energy into this place and did things that may not bring real-world returns, yet truly left marks behind. They brought positive feedback, but also arguments, fatigue, and some unpleasant experiences. Either way, they all took part in shaping who I am now.

Over the years, some people have already met offline. Some have gradually shifted their focus back to study, work, and life. Some only come back once in a while. We may not gather together every day the way we did at the busiest point, but at least we once lived for a while in the same world, and we still exist in one another's memory.

## The Lamp Was Also Shaken by the Wind

::image-grid{class="grid-cols-1" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_6/player_group_20220730_202858.webp","height":"18rem"}]'}
::

> The personality, ability, sense of boundaries, and way of communicating of the people who manage a community will in turn shape that community. This is especially true for small communities. Large organizations can dilute individual style through institutions, but small acquaintance-based communities cannot.

That is something I slowly realized over the past few years. If acquaintance community is the server's base color, then it is not only the players who shape it. Managers are not neutral either. How managers speak, how they handle conflict, who is trusted, who has the right to explain, who gets to make the call, whose emotions get looked after, whose boundaries get protected. All of these things settle bit by bit and become the underlying tone of a community.

Especially for a small community like HydCraft, its earliest organizational shape, the members who joined later, and the personality and ability of the managers themselves all deeply affect what it will become. I once believed rather crudely that if we just rewrote the rules and reorganized the structure, the community could be refactored like a project. Reality later told me it was not that simple.

The inertia of a small circle is often stronger than people imagine. It exists not only in internet servers, but also in real-life classes, dormitories, clubs, and friendships. Who gathered first, who joined later, who is closer to whom, who is used to speaking, who is used to staying silent, who can influence decisions, who always gives way under emotional pressure. These factors are hard to rewrite completely through any single reform. Maybe there really are highly capable people who can reshape a community through more mature management. But for a small server like ours, the ability, energy, and timing that would require are all too great.

Most of the time, all we can do is slowly adjust within already formed relationships and history, slowly patch holes, and slowly learn restraint. If only community atmosphere could be refactored like code. But reality is not like that. Change a few rules, and relationships still keep running along the old inertia. That was another thing I only realized later: atmosphere is not something outside the server, and the initial way a community was organized is not some irrelevant background. Those things are themselves part of the community, and they are almost impossible to fully separate from it.

In the early years, we did not always know how to deal with conflict, and we often mixed friendship, contribution, position, and stance together. Someone could be a friend and also a player. Someone could be a contributor and also a manager. Someone could have given a lot to the server and still hurt others in certain situations. Once identities overlap, many problems become difficult to handle through rules alone. Looking back now, many of the ways we handled things were immature. But perhaps it is precisely because we went through those things that we came to understand the weight of boundaries and restraint more clearly.

Small circles are not naturally pure, either. People can be friends and competitors at the same time. They can learn from one another and still look down on one another. They can hope newcomers will join and still worry that newcomers will disrupt the existing order. They can say they take things casually and still care about how others see them. For a long time, HydCraft was not isolated either. Between us and nearby servers, player groups, and railway-mod circles, there was always some degree of mutual observation, mutual borrowing, and quiet rivalry. Sometimes the website, the Wiki, and the map were not just information and tools, but ways for a small community to present itself to the outside. Those subtle feelings of refusing to lose may not have been mature, but they really did exist.

Of course, when writing about a community, I cannot write only about the people who are still here. The true group portrait of a community should also include those who have already left.

Some people left for their own reasons. Some left because we handled things badly. Some left because both sides were at fault. Some could not fit in. Some faded away gradually. Some did not leave naturally at all, but left amid arguments, disappointment, misunderstanding, and broken relationships. Some people really were hurt at the time, and some also made immature decisions. These are not exactly the parts most fit to be displayed over and over as the face of a server, but we truly did go through them. Whose fault was it? I do not know. It is very hard to reduce every conflict neatly to one person or one cause.

HydCraft is not some ideal community without contradiction. A long-running acquaintance community has warmth, but it also has a cost. When people are close to one another, many things become hard to handle through rules alone. When identities such as player, friend, manager, and contributor overlap, boundaries become blurred too.

These experiences really did change how I understand community, management, and relationships. Over time, I learned that not every problem needs to be solved through complex systems, and not every emotion deserves to be amplified. For a small community to last, what often matters is a small number of people willing to take responsibility, somewhat clearer boundaries, somewhat more restrained expression, and the willingness to keep the lamp lit after the lively days are over. Stay restrained. Stay warm.

## The Other Line

::image-grid{class="grid-cols-1" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_6/player_group_20230720_175842.webp","height":"18rem"}]'}
::

I have talked a lot about changes in the community, so now it is time to talk about technology. In the story of the seasons above, the technical line was really a hidden thread with a weak presence. Most of the space went to stories between people. Technology is productivity. From a broader point of view, the influence of productivity on social structure and social development is self-evident, not to mention in a small community like HydCraft.

At the beginning, the problems were actually simple. How do you get the server running? How do you let players connect? How do you make the client crash less? How do you keep saves from being lost? Home machines, cloud hosts, panel hosts, providers, dynamic IP, backups, client pack integration. These issues kept appearing again and again from Season 1 through Season 3. At the time, my understanding of infrastructure was still very limited, and many lessons were only forced into me after accidents happened.

Looking back now, many early problems were not unsolved because people did not want to solve them. They were unsolved because we genuinely did not yet have the ability. Back then we did not understand server internals, plugins, or compatibility between mods. When trouble came up, all we could usually do was restart, switch providers, reinstall the environment, or guess together in the group chat. Many problems that today look traceable, reproducible, and solvable with tools felt like a black box then.

And the truth is, a server owner is not someone who always knew what to do ahead of time. I certainly was not some genius. More often, the server broke first, players ran into trouble first, someone in the group asked first, and only then did I have to go search for information, read logs, change configs, restart services, and repackage the client. Many things that once looked like generic server instability later turned out to be plugin conflicts, mod compatibility issues, network conditions, permission configuration, or some tiny version mismatch.

Later, the question became how to let more players keep playing stably. Performance and compatibility issues, client rendering problems, map tools, satellite maps, client integration, and mod optimization gradually became part of running the server. What many players remember is not just the buildings themselves, but also the memes and shared experiences born out of technical trouble. CCL, lag, rollbacks, relaunching the client. These were failures on paper, yet they accidentally became part of the shared memory too.

From my point of view, my technical ability was instead slowly pushed forward by HydCraft. Early on, I was mostly just maintaining a server that could run. Only later did I gradually start to understand the relationships among server software, plugins, mods, networking, deployment, backups, and monitoring. Especially with plugin- and mod-related issues, I remained at the level of using and troubleshooting them for a very long time. It was only in recent years, after writing projects myself, doing backend work, wrestling with deployment, and borrowing far more code, that I finally became able to understand some of the problems I could not solve before.

Later still, HydCraft began to need an entry point outside the game. In March 2021, the Wiki gave players a place to record and supplement content freely. In July 2024, Docs began organizing documentation and explanations. In August 2024, status monitoring let us check whether the server was running normally. In 2026, HydCraft Portal became the unified entry point. Player profiles, account binding, identity systems, server status, map entry points, and historical records all helped HydCraft grow from a plain MC save into a more complete community system.

Of course, those web projects themselves did not appear with some grand complete plan from the beginning. Many times they were written only because at some stage it suddenly felt like "there should probably be an entry point," or "this page could look better," or "I could also use this to practice some web work." Some of them dragged on for a long time, and quite a few designs were overturned along the way. They are both infrastructure the server needed and traces left behind in the process of my own technical growth.

So there is no need to imagine HydCraft as something too formal. Write too much about a grand mission and it starts to sound like a project proposal. Much of the time it was not some sacred structure. It was something pieced together bit by bit by middle schoolers, high schoolers, even elementary schoolers and college students, somewhere between real needs, interest, procrastination, sudden bursts of enthusiasm, and late-night page building.

Everyone also has real life, schoolwork, and jobs. Maybe today there is enough energy to write a little, and tomorrow there is not. At the beginning I only wanted to make a page look a bit nicer, and only later did it slowly become a more complete system of records, status, identity, and entry points. The truest reason may be very simple: the server really needed it, so I was forced to learn it bit by bit.

I hope these websites and projects are not just decoration. For a community, records, status, identity, and entry points are themselves part of preserving memory. Maps will restart. Servers will upgrade. Players will leave and return. Group chats will slowly grow cold. But as long as the records are still there, the entry points are still there, and the old screenshots, old pages, old maps, and old documents are still there, the community still has a chance to reconnect itself.

If the cities, railways, and buildings inside the game preserve the traces players left in the save, then these systems outside the server preserve the way the community operates, remembers itself, and passes its past on to those who come later. They may not be flashy, but they make HydCraft more than just a server that was once open. They make it a place that can still be looked up, remembered, and maintained.

## Leaving a Light On for Us

::image-grid{class="grid-cols-1" images='[{"src":"timeline/gallery/season_7/player_group_20250713_005755.webp","height":"18rem"}]'}

HydCraft is not a perfect server. It had a chaotic early period, several lively summers, failed institutional experiments, and many arguments and separations that are hard to write down completely. Many people once took it seriously. It also left some people tired, disappointed, and even willing to walk away.

For newcomers, if there still are newcomers, entering a community that has already existed for many years inevitably takes some time to adapt to. There are old place names, old buildings, old memes, and many habits and tacit understandings that were never written into the rules. We do not want those things to become barriers, but we also have to admit that a community cannot start from zero the way a newly opened server can. People who come later need time to find their place, and we need to keep learning how to leave a clear and friendly enough entry point for them.

But none of that erases the fact that this place existed. People built railways here. People built cities here. People held meetings here, ran activities here, argued here, made up here, left the group here, and returned here. Some brought their classmates into the server. Some brought real-life friends into the group chat. Some taught newcomers how to install the modpack. Some carried screenshots and videos everywhere to promote the place. Some stayed up late debugging code just to keep the server running. Some only came because an acquaintance asked them to take a look, and still ended up leaving a stretch of time here.

Some stayed up late pushing routes forward. Some repeatedly tuned their clients. Some got headaches over renewals, configs, plugins, and mods. Some argued for half a day over the name of a station. Some logged in only to idle. Some talked late into summer voice chats. Some spammed "1" together when the server locked up. These things may look trivial, but they are the most real parts of the server.

Some people also joined for the first time and got completely confused by the group files and announcements, had no idea what they were supposed to download, and could only ask in the group chat one screenshot at a time. Some were pulled in by classmates or acquaintances and originally meant to just take a look, but later still left their own buildings, stations, or place names on the map. Some stayed only briefly. Some gradually stopped logging in. Some barely play MC anymore, yet still remain in the group chat and occasionally ask whether the server is still running.

Some things simply stayed behind. Someone gave a city a name and then stopped coming very often, but that name remained on the map all the same. Someone left, but their buildings stayed. After some people met in real life, the IDs in the group chat suddenly stopped being just IDs. Some people usually said very little, yet when the server had an incident, the map was updated, or an old story came up again, they would suddenly reappear and add one sentence. Many people no longer return often, but a certain route, a certain city, a certain summer voice chat, or some meme that only veteran players understand still ties them gently to this place.

We simply try to keep what is still worth keeping, leave an entry point for people willing to come back, and leave a record for the people who once lived together here. And of course, if anyone still wants to join us, this light will keep making room for new stories too.

After all, the story is still going on.

It is not only HydCraft. Somewhere in the corners of the internet, there are probably many servers, many groups, and many small places where friends and people with shared interests gather together, all going through their own liveliness, chaos, construction, and farewells. There may even still be middle school and high school students like we were in 2018, somewhere in a group chat, on some map, or inside some newly opened server, seriously and clumsily writing down stories of their own.

If you were ever here, I hope that whenever HydCraft comes back to mind, you will know that this place once remembered you seriously. And if you are only passing by, then just treat this as a bit of archaeology from some forgotten corner of the internet. Or pretend I never said any of this. Honestly, the thought that someone might resonate with this little essay of mine is enough to make me excited.
