## Current State

HydCraft is an MC server, mainly built around city building and railway construction. Beyond the server itself, an entire player community has gradually grown around it.

From 2018 to now, HydCraft has gone through seven seasons. In its early days, it felt more like a shared world among friends. Later, it simply happened to grow cities, railways, events, archives, community platforms, status monitoring, and a portal site. It has seen lively periods and chaotic ones alike; there were unforgettable summers, and there were arguments and departures that are still hard to fully put into words.

HydCraft is no longer in the phase where new players keep pouring in. Since Season 7 began in August 2023, large-scale projects have appeared far less often. Many veteran players have moved into new stages of school, work, and everyday life, and the server's activity has shifted from frequent building to quieter companionship. More often than not, people are no longer logging in every day to build. Instead, they chat in group messages, return once in a while to look at the map, bring up an old place name, or reconnect with a world they once maintained together.

In a sense, player counts are more than numbers. They also stand for a group of people who built together, argued, stayed for a while, and still remember one another years later. This page does not record every detail. Given the limits of space, it focuses on how we went from a small multiplayer server to a community that once truly existed, and still exists, in one corner of the internet.

## The Beginning

HydCraft did not start as a server with a complete system, a clear goal, or mature technical foundations. It gradually took shape through repeated season changes, version migrations, players coming and going, and friction within the community. So the best place to start is the beginning.

On September 1, 2018, the earliest form of HydCraft was born from a small MC multiplayer world shared by a few friends. Most of the first players came from group chats around RTM, a train mod. Near the spawn point, players gradually built all kinds of structures, roads, stations, and early urban rail transit, in an area that was later named Binhai City. The server was small, the chat group was small, and the number of daily online players was limited, but the first signs of a transport-focused direction and a habit of building together were already there.

Season 1 lasted only about a month. It was immature and had no clear plan. More than anything else, it was a stretch of raw, uncontrolled growth. The server first ran on a home machine, with unstable bandwidth, hardware, and plugin conditions. Only after moving to a cloud host did the gameplay improve somewhat. This phase was closer to "just playing around." If it had any larger meaning, it was that it formed the earliest player relationships and gave HydCraft its first vague outline as a community.

After Season 2 began, the server gradually moved beyond a simple friends-only multiplayer world and entered a more complicated exploratory phase. The player base grew slowly, and both cities and regions started to expand. Places such as Binhai City, Kunlun City, Xijing City, Fuyuki City, Dongfeng City, Beihai City, and Pingguo City appeared one after another. Calling them cities is only partly accurate. Many of them were closer to personal territories or small-group bases maintained by one or two players over a long period. Each had its own name, routes, architectural style, and social relationships. That is how the server changed from a plain map into a set of places held together by people.

Those regions cooperated, of course, but they also clashed. Much of what looked like relationships between cities was, in reality, still just relationships between players. Who wanted to build roads together, who was willing to connect lines, who disliked someone else's plans, who was closer to whom. These looked like in-game construction issues, but underneath them were the familiar dynamics of cooperation, boundaries, and emotion inside a small acquaintance-based community.

If someone disliked another player's building style, they might throw a few passive-aggressive comments into the group chat. If someone felt their planning had been offended, they might leave the group outright. Looking back, all of it was childish, but it was also real. By then, HydCraft was slowly becoming more than just "let's open a server and play together." It was becoming a community with shared space, player identity, and friction.

Season 2 also exposed how immature the early community still was. Hosts changed frequently, providers were unreliable, mod configurations were repeatedly reworked, external server relationships became a factor, player-made side groups caused disputes, and building aesthetics created splits. For the first time, the server had to face problems created by the community itself. HydCraft was no longer just a small place where a few people played together. It was becoming a small community with outside relationships, internal order, shared resources, and a sense of player identity.

Still, Season 2 did not end through a planned wrap-up. It ended because of a very practical accident: the free hosting provider suddenly disappeared, and the server stopped with it. That stage ultimately came to an end on May 7, 2019.

Season 3 felt more like a short technical transition. After Season 2 was forced to stop, the server moved from 1.7.10 toward 1.12.2. Along the way it went through two world-save replacements and tried new hosting arrangements. After the free provider vanished, one player suggested pooling money to buy a self-managed machine, so for a brief moment the server was no longer fully dependent on free resources.

That attempt did not last long. Two months later, on July 23, the player responsible for the machine could no longer maintain it for personal reasons. The machine had to be sold, and the money was returned. Worse still, during a later system reinstall, the drive was formatted because there was not enough experience with migration and backups, and the Season 3 world save was lost.

Season 3 did not leave behind the most durable building achievements, but it was the first time we truly understood that versioning, hosting, backups, system environments, and maintenance responsibility were not background chores. They were the basic conditions that determined whether a server could survive in the long term. It was also the point where we began to realize that a community could not rely on enthusiasm and luck alone.

Seasons 1 through 3 were HydCraft's first steps. There was a lot of trial and error, and many decisions that now look immature. Still, looked at from the better angle, that immaturity became the earliest set of lessons the server ever had: how to launch a server, how to migrate it, how to respond to player growth, how to handle mod problems, and how to keep the most important things alive through one restart after another.

## Taking Shape

On July 30, 2019, Season 4 began. Compared with the first three seasons, it had more stable resources, broader connections, and a clearer direction. The server kept expanding around railways, cities, and public construction, and it gradually formed a more mature sense of spatial planning.

Even then, the server still had a strong flavor of self-governance among acquaintances. Many cities and regions were not managed by a fully developed institutional framework. They were maintained over time by specific players. Behind a place name, there was often one person, or a few people, along with their taste, their time, their temperament, and their habits of building. On the surface, these places looked like administrative districts. In practice, they worked more like territory defined by agreement between players.

An important turning point in Season 4 came after the start of the pandemic and the shift to online classes in early 2020. The rhythm of everyday life was disrupted, many players suddenly had more time to stay online, and the server entered a rare active phase. This was not unique to HydCraft. Many online communities experienced something similar at the time. People were pushed in front of screens, and it became easier to spend time in virtual spaces, build relationships there, and look for some sense of stability.

That burst of activity also led to more contact between HydCraft and nearby servers. Highways, city construction, administrative reform, and changes to management structure all moved forward one after another. The server gradually started to resemble a "modern server." It was no longer just one city, one railway, or a few familiar territories. It began to form more complicated regional relationships, shared facilities, and a management framework.

There were still conflicts and changes in personnel during this phase, but overall the server started moving in a more stable direction. Players were no longer just logging in temporarily to play. They began investing in long-term construction around cities, roads, railways, and administrative divisions. We also gradually realized that the core of a community is not simply "having a save file." It is having people who are willing to keep investing time, imagination, and relationships into that save file.

Season 4 was the stage where HydCraft truly began to take shape. It carried forward the confusion of the early exploratory years and laid the groundwork for the larger shared memories that came in Season 5.

## A Different Summer

In May 2020, Season 4 ended peacefully after a shared decision. It was HydCraft's second season not to be cut short by a technical accident. On June 7, Season 5 officially began. To a large extent, this season was meant to close out the messier late stage of Season 4 and move the server into a more orderly phase better suited for long-term building.

During that time, HydCraft felt more like a large friends-only server. It never completely left behind the character of an acquaintance-based community, nor did it try to become highly institutionalized like a large public server. More than complex rules or grand planning, what mattered was whether people could have fun together and leave behind their own buildings, routes, and stories. In a way, "fun comes first" was the plainest shared belief of that period.

The importance of Season 5 was not only its larger building scale. For the first time, the server truly accumulated a large body of shared memories. In the summer of 2020, the server saw its first major surge. Then in 2021, after player culture had built up further and another active summer returned, Season 5 hit a second peak. Inspired by the atmosphere of the Tokyo Olympics, the server even held a sports meet. It was hardly rigorous. In fact, it was often chaotic. But that chaos is exactly why so many fragments of it were remembered and repeated later.

The sports meet, the summer peaks, RTM bugs and optimization issues, jokes born from mod quirks, and the growing habits of humor and tacit understanding between players all made Season 5 one of the most memorable periods for many veterans. For a lot of people, it was not the most technically advanced or best planned era, but it was lively and it was joyful. It was like the brightest stretch of summer from an older age. Not mature enough, not stable enough, but easy to remember.

After September 2021, Season 5 gradually entered its late stage, and the server began to quiet down. As player enthusiasm shifted, the version ecosystem stagnated, and calls for a new season grew louder, the old model started to show fatigue. A new server launched under the name "Helium branch server" eventually became the predecessor of Season 6. That also made Season 5 the last main era of HydCraft centered on RTM. Its liveliness, flaws, and warmth were left behind in many people's memories.

It was not the most complete season, but it may be the one people remember most often. It was also the last major phase of HydCraft in the 1.12.2 and RTM era.

## Wind Over the Island

On February 17, 2022, players began proposing a try at MTR, a more modern railway mod. To avoid disrupting the normal operation of Season 5, the new server was first launched under the name "Helium branch server" and ran in parallel with Season 5 for a while. That overlap did not last long. Two months later, Season 5 was shut down because the maintenance cost had become too high, and only the Helium server remained in operation. It was later officially renamed Season 6.

Season 6 upgraded to 1.16.5 and brought in a new mod ecosystem including MTR and Create. That shift also marked HydCraft's move out of the RTM era and into a new stage of construction. Compared with the past, MTR offered a smoother railway experience and gave players a new way to imagine cities, routes, and public transport systems.

Season 6 was one of the busiest and most complicated periods in HydCraft's history. The island map brought cities closer together and made building activity more concentrated. Regions such as Nanyu, Haigang, Hengdan, and Jiuxiang grew quickly. Players kept arriving through videos, publicity, summer activity, and existing social ties, and for a while the server entered a very active construction boom.

This period also carried forward the feel of a large friends-only server. HydCraft was not held together by commercial operation or aggressive traffic acquisition. It was held together by shared interests, social connections, and the relationships between players. Many people joined not only to build railways or cities, but also to take part in a lively, joke-filled, conversational acquaintance-based community where they could leave traces behind. It was not perfect, but it felt lived-in.

The liveliness of Season 6 also made its conflicts much more visible. The island map made it easier for players to build close to one another, which also made spatial conflict more frequent. Railway lines sprawled without much order. Some regions gradually became saturated, and there were even cases where a city refused to let rail lines pass through. Terrain reshaping, project applications, building approvals, and inter-regional relationships stopped being just construction issues and became public matters the server had to take seriously.

Many regions were nominally administrative areas of the server, but in practice they looked more like spheres of influence run over time by different players or small circles. They had their own aesthetics, routes, plans, and social networks, so boundary problems arose naturally. The server wanted to preserve freeform building and a casual atmosphere, yet it also had to deal with overused public space, route conflicts, and approval disputes. On the surface, these arguments were about where a railway should go, whether a building could be approved, or whether terrain could be modified. Underneath, they were the inevitable problems a small acquaintance-based community faces once it scales up: who gets to decide, who should compromise, who can speak for the public interest, and who is simply defending a personal corner of the map.

To ease those problems, we tried building more formal processes, including project review, community platforms, dedicated review bodies, and clearer rules for construction management. Those systems never solved everything. In a small community with a strong acquaintance-based character, too many layers and approvals that are too rigid can easily turn a "building problem" into a "people problem." Some institutional experiments were meant to reduce chaos, but in practice they created new friction of their own.

That made Season 6 an important governance experiment for HydCraft. We gradually learned that a small community does not necessarily suit complex institutions, and approvals alone cannot solve every issue. What matters more may be a small number of trusted people, clear boundaries, timely communication, and restraint in handling conflict. Rules are necessary, but rules cannot replace trust, and they cannot replace real people taking responsibility.

Season 6 also produced a lot of distinctive server culture. Compatibility problems between MTR and OptiFine gave rise to the meme later known as "CCL": when a player walked into certain locations, the client could suddenly produce a rendering error and stop drawing chunks until the game was restarted. Even more absurdly, people would sometimes imitate those error messages on purpose just to make others think they had run into CCL again.

The collective flood of "1" in chat after a WorldEdit command froze the server's main thread also became part of that era's way of making fun out of frustration. It was not just noise for its own sake. When the main thread froze, movement, block updates, and most in-game actions stopped, but chat could sometimes still go through. So players kept sending "1" just to check whether the server was still alive. If chat still appeared, at least that meant the server was not fully dead yet. If even chat stopped, it usually meant the save file might no longer be recoverable in time, and rollback was now the real concern.

This period also had what we called "discussion meetings." In practice, those were just long Tencent Meeting calls where everyone talked about the future of the server while inevitably drifting off topic again and again. At the time, we once believed that if meetings like that became routine, they would produce a better self-governing order. We also imagined that one large, comprehensive management framework could finally resolve all the tensions that had built up over time.

Looking back now, that idea was naive. It overlooked a set of practical realities: small communities have limited manpower, acquaintance networks are complicated, and the cost of execution is high. Those meetings did not always produce many concrete outcomes, and often they felt more like a group of players sincerely but casually imagining what the server might become. Even so, they were not meaningless. At least at that moment, people still believed the server would keep growing, stay lively, and continue generating new stories.

By the summer of 2023, as saturation in building, friction between small circles, and disagreement over the server's direction all broke out at once, Season 6 gradually reached the end of its cycle. The launch of Season 7 was both a new technical and cartographic attempt and a response to the problems that surfaced late in Season 6.

Later on, Season 6 was reopened from time to time. Perhaps it never became entirely part of the past. It was not HydCraft's most stable period, but it may have been one of the most crowded, the most vivid, and the clearest expressions of how complicated an acquaintance-based community can be.

## Still Lit, Slowly

On August 7, 2023, in the aftermath of the conflicts that broke out in Season 6, Season 7 officially began. It used a player-drawn map, and the person who led that map work later became the current vice owner. At the same time, the server upgraded to 1.18.2 and adopted a higher-version mod ecosystem. Compared with Season 6, Season 7 brought new advantages in map design, mod variety, and modern-version experience. It also inherited the late-Season-6 discussion around building space, organizational structure, and future direction. On December 31, 2025, Season 7 was upgraded to 1.20.1 while keeping the existing save file.

The launch of Season 7 had a strong player-organized character. Map design, test servers, client integration, and early preparation all depended on players pushing things forward themselves. It was not fully planned from the top down by a management team. It was a new stage brought into being by a group of players who were still willing to keep building, in the middle of the conflict, stagnation, and fatigue that marked late Season 6.

But after Season 7 began, the overall pace of the server clearly slowed down. Many veteran players gradually entered new phases of school, work, and real life, and the time they could invest in MC construction decreased. Season 7 had a larger map and a newer version ecosystem, but it also faced new problems. The scale of the map made building feel scattered. There were too few people consistently driving long-term projects, and too few new players to replenish that energy. The server was no longer steadily lively in the way it had been during the summer of 2022.

That does not mean HydCraft is over. Perhaps we simply moved from being a high-frequency building community into a low-frequency companionship community. The current HydCraft does not always prove itself through daily player counts anymore. It continues instead through the relationships that still remain between old players, the saves that are still preserved, the Wiki that is still maintained, and the memories that are still brought up from time to time in group chat.

From 2023 to now, Season 7 has not seen many earth-shaking events. It has been, more than anything, a calm period of long-term maintenance. Sometimes someone returns for a look. Sometimes something new goes online. Sometimes there is another discussion about administrative divisions, server status, or whether to build something new in the future. It is not as lively as Seasons 5 and 6, but it is closer to what the latter half of a real long-term community often looks like. People may not show up every day, but they have not truly left either.

If the early seasons looked like clear intervals with beginnings and endings, then present-day HydCraft feels more like a curve that has not fully converged yet. It no longer changes violently every day, and it has no clearly written endpoint. It simply keeps extending forward at a slow pace, sometimes fluctuating, sometimes pausing, and sometimes changing a little more because one person comes back. It is no longer a loud summer. It is more like a lamp that is still on. Not blinding, not noisy, but enough to let people know this place is still here.

## An Acquaintance-Based Community

HydCraft has always carried the traits of an acquaintance-based community. Or rather, most long-running MC servers probably do, at least if our own path says anything. Early on, the server gathered around friendships and shared interests. In the middle years, it expanded through building, events, summer activity, and word of mouth. Later, it depended more on the long-term connections between a small group of core players.

That structure gave HydCraft a lot of warmth. Players were never just strangers to one another. Many place names, lines, buildings, and memes are tied to specific people. A railway, a station, a memorial building, or a map inside the server is often more than infrastructure. It is a trace left behind by relationships and time.

That is also why the "cities" inside HydCraft were never just administrative labels on a map. Many of them were really just living ranges maintained over time by one player or a small group of players. They were like cities, but also like territory; like administrative zones, but also like circles of acquaintances. Behind a place name, there might be a route plan, an architectural taste, several friendships, or an argument from long ago.

We also formed a lot of unique server culture. RTM and RailCraft gave the early years their railway flavor. The sports meet of Season 5 became the first batch of shared memories that kept getting brought up again. In Season 6, CCL bugs, WorldEdit freezes, summer construction booms, player-made assets, and community platforms spread that culture further. None of those memes were grand in scale, but only people who stayed a long time could really understand them.

Of course, an acquaintance-based community has its costs. When people are close, emotions affect one another more easily. As the player base grows, issues around small circles, boundaries, and shared resources become more likely. Too little structure leads to chaos; too much structure turns rigid. We tried adjusting rules, reviews, organization, and management in different periods, but not every attempt worked. In particular, during the rough expansion of Season 6, some decisions and some ways of handling small-circle problems were far from satisfying.

Many people did not join HydCraft through any formal recruitment at all. Some were brought in by classmates, some by real-life friends, some were invited in after getting to know people in other group chats, and some just saw screenshots, videos, or casual conversation and thought it looked interesting enough to check out. Some stayed. Some only played briefly. Some went from player to chat-group regular, and some went from chat-group regular to just another name in memory.

People do not stay in one place forever, though. In practice, someone can start drifting away on any given day. Sometimes a person suddenly stops talking, and only a week later does anyone notice. Some no longer play MC, but still remain in the group chat. Others never quite find a place in the community, become quieter over time, and eventually fade from view.

There was a time when I found that kind of departure hard to accept. For a while, every time someone left the group or fell silent, I took it seriously and kept asking why they had left, why they no longer spoke, or why they would not stay. Looking back now, that kind of questioning was not very necessary, and it was not always fair. In a long-running community, if some people come closer, others will leave. Not every departure needs an explanation, and not every silence means failure.

Seen from today, the most suitable form for HydCraft may not be a complex institution at all. It may be long-term maintenance with low burden, few layers, strong trust, and clear boundaries. A small number of people who are truly willing to take responsibility keep basic order in place. Within that space, players can build and chat freely, and when problems appear, they are handled with as much restraint as possible instead of forcing every relationship through institutional friction.

That is also why the small group of people who are still active now matters so much. At present, more than thirty regular players remain active in the inner group. For a large community, thirty people may sound insignificant. For a long-running small community like HydCraft, those people are exactly why the server still has a voice, still has conversations, and still gets remembered.

People have said that HydCraft has a good atmosphere. I think that is probably not only because the rules are complete, the websites look good, or the server is stable enough. It is because there is still a group of people willing to remain here in a gentler, lower-noise way. They may not log in every day, and there may not always be new construction results, but as long as people are still talking in the group chat, the save files are still preserved, the Wiki is still maintained, and old stories are still brought up from time to time, HydCraft has not truly disappeared.

At least for me, that also proves that I was never entertaining myself alone. I invested a great deal of time and energy here, and I did things that may never bring direct real-world rewards but did leave real traces behind. Some of those things brought positive feedback. Some brought arguments, fatigue, and unpleasant experiences. Either way, they all helped shape who I am now.

Over the years, some people have already met offline, some have gradually shifted their focus back to study, work, and life, and some only return once in a while. We may no longer gather together every day the way we did at the busiest point, but we did spend part of our lives in the same world, and we still remain in one another's memory.

## The Other Line

The changes in HydCraft did not happen only inside the game world. They also happened outside the server itself.

At first, the problems were simple. How do you get the server running? How do you let players connect? How do you make the client crash less? How do you keep world saves from being lost? Home machines, cloud hosts, panel-based hosting, providers, dynamic IP, backups, and client pack integration kept coming up again and again from Seasons 1 to 3. At that point our understanding of infrastructure was still limited, and many lessons were only learned after something had already gone wrong.

Very often, a server owner is not someone who already knows what to do. More often, the server breaks first, players run into trouble first, somebody asks about it in chat first, and only then do you start searching for information, reading logs, changing configuration, restarting services, and repackaging the client. Many things that once looked like generic server instability later turned out to be plugin conflicts, mod compatibility, network conditions, permission configuration, or tiny version differences.

Looking back now, many early problems were not unsolved because no one wanted to solve them. They were unsolved because we genuinely did not yet know how. At that time we did not understand server internals, plugins, or mod compatibility. When trouble came up, the usual options were restarting, switching providers, reinstalling the environment, or guessing with each other in chat. Many issues that today seem traceable, reproducible, and toolable felt like a black box back then.

Later, the question became how to let more players keep playing in a stable way. RTM performance and compatibility issues, MTR rendering issues on the client side, the impact of WorldEdit on the main thread, map tools, satellite maps, integrated client packs, and mod optimization all gradually became part of operating the server. Many players remember not only the buildings themselves, but also the jokes and shared experiences produced by technical problems. CCL, server lag, rollbacks, and relaunching the client were all failures on paper, yet they accidentally became part of the shared memory too.

From the perspective of the owner, this was also how HydCraft kept pushing my technical ability forward. In the early years I was mostly just maintaining a server that could stay online. Only later did I begin to understand the relationships between server software, plugins, mods, networking, deployment, backups, and monitoring. For a long time, especially with plugin and mod issues, I could only stay at the level of usage and troubleshooting. It was only in recent years, after building projects of my own, doing backend work, wrestling with deployment, and reading and reusing much more code, that I finally became able to understand some of the problems I could not solve before.

Later still, HydCraft needed an entry point outside the game. The Wiki let players record and supplement content freely. Docs organized documents and explanations. Status monitoring made it possible to check whether the server was running normally. The Portal served as a unified front door. Player profiles, account linking, identity systems, server status, map entry points, and historical records also helped HydCraft grow from a plain MC save file into a more complete community system.

Of course, those web projects were not well made from the start either. I made plenty of mistakes in them. I wrote structures that were not mature enough, created designs I later wanted to tear down and rebuild, and ran into all kinds of problems around deployment, permissions, interfaces, caching, CORS, proxies, and account systems. But precisely because HydCraft kept generating real needs, it also gave me a real environment in which to keep training technical ability. A lot of things were not learned first and then built. They were learned because the server genuinely needed them.

I do not want those sites and projects to be mere decoration. For a long-running community, records, status, identity, and entry points are themselves part of how memory is preserved. Maps restart. Servers upgrade. Players leave and come back. Group chats grow quiet. But if the records still exist, if the entry points still exist, if the old screenshots, old pages, old maps, and old documents still exist, then the community still has a chance to reconnect with itself.

If the cities, railways, and buildings inside the game preserve the traces of players inside the world save, then the systems outside the server preserve something else: how the community runs, how it remembers itself, and how it hands the past over to those who come later. They may not be the most visible parts, but they are what make HydCraft more than a server that was once open. They make it a place that can still be looked up, remembered, and maintained.

## Leaving a Light On

HydCraft is not a perfect server, of course. It had a chaotic early phase, several lively summers, failed institutional experiments, and many arguments and separations that are hard to fully write down. Many people once treated it seriously. It also exhausted some people, disappointed some people, and even pushed some people to leave.

But none of that erases the fact that it existed. People built railways here. People built cities here. People held meetings here, ran events here, argued here, reconciled here, left the group here, and came back here. Some pulled in classmates. Some brought real-life friends into the group chat. Some taught newcomers how to install the modpack. Some took screenshots and videos everywhere to spread the word. Some stayed up late changing code just to keep the server running. Some were only called over by an acquaintance to take a look and still ended up leaving part of their time here.

Some stayed up late pushing rail lines forward. Some repeatedly tuned client setups. Some argued half a day over the name of a station. Some logged in only to show others around the cities they had built. Some talked deep into summer voice chats. Some spammed "1" together when the server froze. Some got headaches from renewal fees, configuration, plugins, and mods. Some left behind maps, assets, documents, and pages they created themselves. Some stayed only briefly. Others left a long stretch of their youth in this server.

Some people did not even know how to install the modpack the first time they joined, and could only ask question after question in chat with screenshots. Some were dragged in by classmates or acquaintances and originally meant to just look around, but still ended up leaving their own house, station, or place name on the map. Some have not logged in for a long time, yet still suddenly ask whether the old save file is still there. Some open the web map just to see whether the place they once built still remains. Some hardly play MC anymore, yet they still remember a route, a city, a summer voice call, or a meme that only old players understand.

HydCraft is no longer at its busiest. Still, I think it is more like a lamp that remains on. We do not ask everyone to come back every day, and we do not pretend the past will never end. We simply try to keep what is still worth keeping, leave an entry point for the people who want to return, and leave a record for the people who once lived together here. And if someone still wants to join us, that light can keep making room for new stories too.

This page is dedicated to recording the fact that HydCraft once existed, and still exists, in one corner of the internet, and to recording the people who left behind their names, buildings, routes, and memories here.
